Topics/Trade & tariffs/Who pays for tariffs?
Claim Claim #TR-04 · Topic: Trade & tariffs · Election relevance: High

“Foreign countries pay the cost of U.S. tariffs, not Americans.”

Where you hear it: campaign speeches, cable news segments, and social posts describing tariffs as a tax on China, Mexico, or Europe rather than on U.S. buyers. The economic question underneath: when the U.S. puts a tariff on imports, who ends up paying it?
Our assessment
Not supported. The evidence shows U.S. buyers paid nearly the entire cost of the 2018–2019 tariffs.
For the newer 2025–2026 tariff rounds the evidence base is still forming; early studies point the same direction but with less precision. Section 4 explains how we grade this.

The 30-second version

A tariff is a tax collected at the U.S. border on imported goods. In theory, who ultimately pays depends on whether foreign sellers cut their prices to stay competitive. Several independent studies of the 2018–2019 tariffs, using detailed customs and price data, found that foreign sellers mostly did not cut prices: U.S. import prices rose by roughly the full amount of the tariff, so the cost landed on U.S. importers, businesses, and consumers. Some of it showed up in store prices right away (washing machines are the clearest case); some was absorbed by U.S. firms’ profit margins first. There is real disagreement about the size of the long-run effects on jobs and manufacturing, but not about who paid the tariff itself.

What is the policy trying to address?

Tariffs are proposed for several distinct goals, and the “foreigners pay” claim matters differently for each: raising federal revenue without taxing Americans directly; protecting domestic manufacturing jobs from import competition; pressuring trading partners in negotiations; and reducing dependence on specific countries for strategic goods. This page grades only the first-order question of who bears the cost. Related claims about jobs, revenue, and national security each have their own page.

What does economic theory say?

Theory does not settle this question; it tells us what to look for. A tariff drives a wedge between the price a foreign seller receives and the price a U.S. buyer pays. How that wedge is split is called incidence, and it depends on bargaining power: if the U.S. is a large enough buyer that foreign sellers will cut prices to keep the market, foreigners absorb some of the tariff (the “terms-of-trade” effect). If foreign sellers can easily sell elsewhere, or if U.S. buyers have few substitutes, U.S. buyers absorb it.

Hypothesized effect
Import prices rise by between 0% (foreigners pay all) and 100% (Americans pay all) of the tariff. The empirical question is where on that line we land.
Trade-offs
Even if Americans pay, tariffs can still achieve other goals (protection, revenue, leverage). The cost is a reason to weigh those goals, not a reason they fail.
Unintended consequences
Higher input costs for U.S. manufacturers that use imported parts; retaliation against U.S. exporters (farm goods especially); supply-chain rerouting through third countries.

What does the empirical literature say?

The 2018–2019 tariffs were unusually well studied because they were large, sudden, and product-specific, which lets researchers compare prices of tariffed goods against similar untariffed goods before and after. Every study included below is a causal design; descriptive and opinion pieces were screened out (see Methods). What is still unknown?

  • Whether very broad tariffs (covering most trading partners at once) change exporter pricing behavior relative to the targeted 2018 tariffs.
  • How much of the cost absorbed in U.S. firms’ margins eventually reaches consumers, and over what horizon.
  • The long-run effect on domestic investment and plant openings, which short event-study windows cannot capture.
  • Distributional incidence: which households, by income and region, bear the largest share.
6

Where do experts disagree, and why?

Disagreement here is mostly not about the incidence finding. It is about what follows from it. Economists who favor tariffs generally accept that Americans paid for the 2018 round and argue the cost is worth paying for strategic or industrial-policy reasons that are outside the scope of this claim. A smaller group argues that pass-through estimates from 2018 overstate long-run incidence because supply chains had not yet adjusted. We classify this as a disagreement about time horizon and policy goals, not about the data.

What would change our assessment
Two or more peer-reviewed studies of the 2025–26 tariffs finding border pass-through well below 100% over a multi-year window, or evidence of sustained exporter price cuts in customs data.
