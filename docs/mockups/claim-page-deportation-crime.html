Topics/Immigration/Do deportations reduce crime?
Claim Claim #IM-02 · Topic: Immigration · Election relevance: High

“Deporting more immigrants makes our communities safer.”

Where you hear it: campaign rallies, local-news coverage of enforcement operations, and posts tying deportation numbers to crime rates. The economic question underneath: when interior immigration enforcement increases, does crime in the affected communities fall?
Our assessment
Not supported. The best studies of the largest U.S. interior-enforcement program found no detectable reduction in violent or property crime.
That evidence comes mostly from Secure Communities (2008–2014), which removed people after arrest. The 2025–2026 enforcement expansion is larger and less targeted, and no causal study of its crime effects has yet been published; we cannot grade it. Section 4 explains.

The 30-second version

The cleanest evidence comes from Secure Communities, a federal program that rolled out county by county between 2008 and 2014 and led to hundreds of thousands of removals. Because counties joined at different times, researchers could compare crime trends in counties that had just activated against those that had not yet. Several independent teams found no measurable drop in violent or property crime, and some found side effects: fewer immigrants reporting crimes, lower police clearance rates, and reduced use of public benefits by eligible citizen children. Whether today’s larger, less targeted enforcement has different effects is an open question that no causal study has yet answered. The claim that immigrants commit more crime than the native-born is a separate claim with its own page, and the evidence there points the other way.

What is the policy trying to address?

Interior enforcement (identifying and removing people already living in the U.S. without authorization) is justified on several grounds that this page keeps separate: public safety, which is the claim tested here; the rule of law, a values argument we do not grade; labor-market effects on native workers, which has its own page; and fiscal costs. Public-safety claims usually bundle two different statements: that immigrants commit more crime, and that removing them reduces crime. The first is a separate claim with its own page. This page tests only the second.

What does economic theory say?

Theory gives reasons to expect crime to fall and reasons to expect it to rise, and the net sign is an empirical question. Removing people who would otherwise commit crimes reduces crime directly (incapacitation), and the threat of removal may deter offending by those who remain (deterrence). But the size of both effects depends on how many of the people removed would have offended, and that number is small if immigrants offend at rates at or below the native-born. Working against those channels: enforcement can make immigrant communities less willing to report crimes or cooperate as witnesses, which lowers the chance that offenders are caught and can raise crime against immigrants specifically.

Hypothesized effect
Ambiguous sign. A fall in crime requires that the people removed had a meaningful offending rate; a rise is possible if reduced cooperation with police outweighs incapacitation.
Trade-offs
Even with no crime effect, enforcement may serve other goals (rule of law, labor-market aims). The cost side includes enforcement spending, lost output and tax revenue, and effects on citizen family members.
Unintended consequences
Lower crime reporting and police clearance in immigrant neighborhoods; reduced benefit take-up by eligible citizen children; local labor shortages in agriculture and construction.

What does the empirical literature say?

Secure Communities is the main source of credible evidence because its staggered county rollout created a natural experiment. A second, smaller literature studies 287(g) agreements, under which local police enforce immigration law, and a third studies sanctuary policies, which are the reverse treatment. Every study included below has a causal design; descriptive comparisons of crime rates across cities were screened out (see Methods). What can we conclude, and how sure are we?

The claim is not supported for the 2008–2014 generation of interior enforcement. Our certainty is Moderate, not High, for two reasons visible in the criteria below: almost all the strong evidence comes from a single program, and that program differs from current enforcement in scale and targeting. For 2025–26 enforcement the claim cannot be graded: no study with a causal design has been published, and we say what evidence would be needed.

What is still unknown?

  • Whether enforcement at the 2025–26 scale, with less targeting on people arrested for crimes, has effects that the Secure Communities evidence cannot predict in either direction.
  • How much measured crime changes because reporting changes: if immigrants report less, recorded crime can fall while actual victimization does not. Victimization surveys, not police data, are needed to separate the two.
  • Effects on specific crime categories (for example, crimes with immigrant victims) that county aggregates may hide.
  • Long-run effects on neighborhoods after large removals, including on the children of those removed.
6

Where do experts disagree, and why?

The null finding on Secure Communities is not seriously disputed among researchers who study it. Disagreement is about scope (whether findings from a post-arrest program transfer to broad enforcement) and data (whether police-recorded crime is the right outcome when enforcement itself changes reporting). A separate public disagreement, about whether immigrants commit more crime, is a different claim and is handled on its own page. We classify the live disagreement here as external validity and measurement, not as a dispute about the Secure Communities results.

Steelman: “deportations make us safer”
The strongest version: Secure Communities removed relatively few people per county per year, so a real but small incapacitation effect could be undetectable in county crime aggregates; removals targeted at people with serious convictions may reduce specific crimes even if total crime does not move; and large-scale enforcement could have deterrence effects that a gradual program never tested. What would change our assessment
For the 2025–26 generation: two or more studies with a credible design (variation in enforcement intensity across places or time) using both police-recorded crime and victimization data. A finding of reduced crime in those studies would move the verdict for the current generation to Mixed or Supported; we would then split the page by generation.
