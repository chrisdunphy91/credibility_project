Topics/Immigration/Are immigrants taking American jobs?
Claim Claim #IM-01 · Topic: Immigration · Election relevance: High

“Immigrants are taking jobs from American workers.”

Where you hear it: campaign ads, debate exchanges, and posts connecting immigration numbers to unemployment or factory closures. The economic question underneath: when more immigrants join the U.S. labor force, do U.S.-born workers end up with fewer jobs, or lower pay?
Our assessment · two parts
Jobs
Not supported. Across the major U.S. natural experiments, immigration does not reduce the number of jobs held by U.S.-born workers, and removing or excluding immigrants has not created jobs for them.
Wages of the closest competitors
Mixed. Whether low-skill immigration lowers pay for U.S.-born workers without a high-school diploma is a real dispute among economists, with estimates from modestly negative to roughly zero.
The public claim is about jobs. The live academic dispute is about wages for one group. We grade them separately so neither can be used to settle the other. Section 4 explains.

The 30-second version

The number of jobs in an economy is not fixed. Immigrants fill jobs, but they also spend money, start businesses, and take on tasks that let U.S.-born workers move into different roles, so the net effect on native employment is an empirical question. The U.S. has run that experiment several times: a sudden influx (the 1980 Mariel boatlift to Miami), a sudden exclusion (ending the Bracero farm-worker program in 1964), tight quotas (the 1920s), and mass removals (the 1930s repatriations and the 2008–2014 Secure Communities program). In none of them did U.S.-born workers gain jobs when immigrants were kept out or removed, and in several they lost some. Where economists do disagree is on wages for the small group of U.S.-born workers who compete most directly with low-skill immigrants, where estimates range from a modest decline to no change. That dispute is real, but it is not the same claim as “taking jobs.”

1

What is the policy trying to address?

Restricting immigration, or removing immigrants already here, is often justified as protecting U.S.-born workers from competition for jobs and from downward pressure on pay. This page tests that justification. It does not test the fiscal, cultural, or rule-of-law arguments for restriction, which are either separate claims or values questions outside our scope. The tested statement covers both legal and unauthorized immigration, because the labor-market mechanism is the same; where a study isolates one, the table says so.

2

What does economic theory say?

The simplest model says more workers competing for a fixed set of jobs lowers wages or employment for those already there. Economists call the fixed-jobs assumption the “lump of labor” fallacy, because labor demand is not fixed: immigrants consume goods and services, which creates jobs; firms expand and invest when labor is available; and immigrants and natives often do different tasks, so natives shift toward communication-intensive and supervisory work whose pay rises. Theory therefore predicts small or zero effects on total native employment in the long run, with any negative effects concentrated on the workers who are closest substitutes for new arrivals, and even those effects fading as capital adjusts.

Hypothesized effect
Near-zero effect on total native employment; possible short-run wage declines for close substitutes (prior immigrants and native dropouts); gains for complements (higher-skill natives, employers, consumers).
Trade-offs
Aggregate gains can coexist with losses for a specific group. The policy question is distributional: who gains, who loses, and whether the losers are compensated.
Unintended consequences of restriction
Employers mechanize, switch crops or products, offshore, or shrink rather than hire natives at higher pay; complementary native jobs disappear with the immigrant jobs they depended on.

What can we conclude, and how sure are we?

On jobs, the claim is not supported at High certainty. Four separate exclusion or removal episodes spanning a century, studied by different teams with strong designs, all fail to find native job gains, and the inflow experiments find no employment losses. On wages for the closest competitors, the verdict is Mixed at Moderate certainty. Well-designed studies reach different answers, and the disagreement is partly about method (national cells versus local experiments) and partly about one contested dataset. What is still unknown?

  • The size of the wage effect on native workers without a high-school diploma, who are now a small and shrinking share of the labor force, and how long it lasts.
  • Whether the 2021–2024 immigration surge, the largest in decades, affected native employment and wages; studies are only beginning to appear and the search is rerun monthly.
  • Effects on specific occupations and regions that national and metro-level studies may average away.
  • How much of the long-run adjustment comes from natives moving to different jobs versus different places, which matters for who bears the transition cost.
6

Where do experts disagree, and why?

This is one of the sharpest and longest-running disputes in empirical economics, and it is worth being precise about what it is and is not about. It is not about whether immigrants reduce the number of jobs held by natives; the Borjas and Card camps both find that they do not. It is about the wage effect on the lowest-skill natives, and it turns on two things: method (national skill-cell studies that assume a fixed economy-wide labor demand find larger effects than local natural experiments that let demand respond), and data (the Mariel re-analysis hinges on a subsample of a few hundred survey respondents whose composition changed over the period). We classify this as a method-and-data disagreement about magnitude for one group, not a dispute about the headline claim.

Steelman: “immigrants take American jobs”
The strongest version: in the short run, before firms and workers adjust, a large local inflow lowers pay for the natives who compete most directly, and some of those natives leave the labor force or the area rather than take lower pay, which local studies can miss by counting only those who stay. National studies that track skill groups wherever they live find wage declines of a few percent for dropouts. Even if total native employment is unchanged, the people displaced are real and are not the people who gain. Evidence: Borjas 2003 and 2017; Monras 2020 on short-run effects; [to be filled: native out-migration studies].
What would change our assessment
On jobs: two or more credible studies of the 2021–2024 surge, or of 2025–26 removals, finding sustained native employment losses or gains respectively. On wages: a resolution of the Mariel data dispute through independent data, or two or more studies with credible designs placing the dropout wage effect clearly in one half of the current range. Either would move a grade; only the first would move a verdict.

Why two verdicts
“Taking jobs” and “lowering wages for some workers” are different claims with different evidence. Our rules (Protocol section 3.4) require splitting them so that a contested finding about one group’s pay cannot be used to support, or dismiss, a claim about everyone’s jobs.
